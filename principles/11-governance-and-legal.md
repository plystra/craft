# Governance and Legal

## 1. Governance philosophy

Plystra should grow governance gradually.

Do not imitate the full structure of a foundation before there is a real contributor base, maintenance burden, or legal need. But do build habits that make future governance possible.

Good early governance means:

- decisions are written down;
- licenses are clear;
- ownership is explicit;
- project status is honest;
- contribution rules are understandable;
- security reports have a path;
- public claims are careful.

## 2. Ownership and attribution

Every project under Plystra should clearly state its ownership and operating context.

Recommended wording where appropriate:

```text
Plystra is a trademark operated by Zhizhizhi (Shanghai) Technology Co., Ltd.
```

Use the exact legal wording only where it is accurate for the specific public surface. Do not place legal claims in a repository if the relationship is uncertain.

## 3. Licenses

Every public repository must have an explicit license.

The license should match the project's goals:

- permissive licenses for broad adoption and developer tools;
- copyleft licenses only when reciprocal sharing is an intentional goal;
- source-available terms only when open-source status is not being claimed;
- proprietary terms when the project is not open source.

Do not call a project open source unless its license is an approved open-source license.

## 4. Contributor expectations

If outside contributions are accepted, the repository should include contribution rules.

Contribution rules should explain:

- how to open issues;
- how to propose changes;
- coding and documentation standards;
- testing expectations;
- review process;
- license implications;
- behavior expectations.

Keep the tone direct and respectful. Avoid overly corporate language.

## 5. Decision records

Use decision records for meaningful choices.

A decision record should explain:

- context;
- decision;
- options considered;
- consequences;
- review date if relevant.

Decision records are not bureaucracy. They protect future maintainers from rediscovering old reasoning.

## 6. Public claims

Legal and public claims must be conservative.

Avoid claims such as:

- `secure` without a defined standard;
- `private` without explaining data handling;
- `compliant` without naming the framework and scope;
- `enterprise-grade` without evidence;
- `open source` without a qualifying license;
- `official` when the project is not formally endorsed by an external organization.

## 7. Trademarks and naming

Project names should be checked before public release.

A naming review should consider:

- obvious conflicts;
- domain availability;
- repository availability;
- pronunciation;
- spelling;
- cultural meaning;
- whether the name can mature with the product.

Do not choose names that depend on another company's mark unless the project is clearly an integration and the usage is nominative.

## 8. Privacy and terms pages

Public products that collect user data should eventually provide:

- privacy policy;
- terms of use or service terms;
- contact path;
- data deletion instructions;
- third-party processor disclosure where relevant.

Early private projects may use simpler notices, but the direction should be clear before public launch.

## 9. Governance maturity

Plystra governance may evolve through stages:

### Stage 0: Personal stewardship

One maintainer controls direction. Decisions are still documented.

### Stage 1: Maintainer-led projects

Projects may have contributors. Maintainer roles and review expectations are documented.

### Stage 2: Shared project governance

Multiple maintainers can approve changes. Release and security responsibilities are assigned.

### Stage 3: Foundation-like stewardship

Project admission, retirement, trademark use, and governance are formalized.

Do not skip stages for appearance.

## 10. Legal review checklist

Before public launch of a Plystra project:

1. Is the license explicit?
2. Is ownership clear?
3. Are trademark statements accurate?
4. Are third-party licenses compatible?
5. Are public claims supportable?
6. Is user data handled according to the published policy?
7. Are examples free of secrets and private infrastructure details?
8. Is there a security reporting path?
